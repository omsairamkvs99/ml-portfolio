The ML Algorithm Framework

Twelve machine learning algorithms, sorted by how they learn and where they are actually used. I built this to answer a question I kept running into at work: when a problem shows up, which family of models even makes sense to look at? Click any algorithm in the framework, the domain map, or the connections section to jump to its full breakdown.

Layer 2 · Application Domain

02Domain map

Each row is an algorithm and each column is one of the four domains. A filled circle marks the domain an algorithm was built for or is mainly used in. A ring marks a domain where it shows up regularly but is not the first choice.

What the map shows: the classical models stack up in the tabular column, while the deep learning models spread across several domains. Generative AI is the clearest pattern of all. Every model that has generative AI as its primary domain learns without human labels, which is a big reason these models could scale so fast, since they can train on raw images and text without anyone labeling it first.

Choosing the categories

The assignment gave me two learning styles and four domains, so I treated them as two separate layers instead of trying to force everything into one list. Learning style describes how a model is trained, while domain describes what kind of problem it solves. Keeping those apart is what makes the framework useful, because two models can learn the same way and still belong to completely different domains, like XGBoost and CNNs, which are both supervised.

For learning style, I sorted by the source of the training signal rather than by the task. If a model needs a person to supply the correct answer for each example, it went under supervised. If it learns only from the raw data, it went under unsupervised. For domains, I separated a primary domain from secondary ones. Almost any model can be bent to fit any data type, so marking everything as "used in all four" would have been technically defensible but useless for choosing a model.

The edge cases I had to decide

Self supervised models. Transformers like GPT and diffusion models do not fit cleanly into either bucket. They train on unlabeled data, but they create their own targets, such as the next word or the noise that was added to an image. I placed them under the unsupervised branch, since no human labeling is involved, but gave them their own dashed group so the distinction stays visible.
Transformers after pretraining. Once a transformer is fine tuned on labeled data, that stage is supervised. I classified it by its pretraining stage, because that is where nearly all of its knowledge comes from, and I noted the fine tuning step on its card.
GANs and the discriminator. A GAN's discriminator technically learns a real versus fake label, which looks supervised at first. Those labels come from the training setup, not from a person, so the model as a whole stays unsupervised.
CNNs and generative AI. I marked generative AI as a secondary domain for CNNs because convolution layers sit inside most image generators, even though a CNN on its own is a classifier.

What the sorting showed me

Once everything was placed, a pattern showed up that I did not expect going in. The learning style and the domain are closely tied. Supervised models dominate tabular data because businesses already have labeled history, like past fraud cases or past sales. Generative AI sits almost entirely on the unsupervised side because there is far more unlabeled content in the world than labeled content. That pushed me to think about classification less as a list of facts and more as a reflection of what data is actually available for a problem.
