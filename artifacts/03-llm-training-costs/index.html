What It Really Takes to Train a Large Language Model

Behind every chatbot answer is a training run that consumed trillions of words, tens of thousands of chips, and enough electricity to power a small town. This page follows that process from raw data to deployment and puts real numbers on each resource, using GPT, Llama, Gemini, Claude, and Grok as examples.

Part 1 · The training process

01From raw text to a deployed model

Training an LLM happens in stages. The meters on each step show how heavily it draws on each resource, from one bar (light) to four bars (dominant). Pretraining is where most of the compute, energy, and money goes, but the steps around it decide whether the model is actually useful and safe.

Part 2 · The resources

02Where the money and energy go

Four resources set the price of a model: data, compute, energy, and time, with people running through all of them. Each one also shapes how capable the final model is.

Resource 1

Data

Models learn from text measured in tokens, pieces of words. Training sets have grown about fifty times in four years, and the best text is increasingly licensed rather than free.

Impact on the model: more and cleaner data improves knowledge and reasoning, and the mix decides what the model is good at.

Data also costs money directly: OpenAI's 2024 content deal with News Corp was reported to be worth more than $250 million over five years.

Resource 2

Compute

Training compute is roughly 6 × parameters × tokens floating point operations. That math runs on thousands of GPUs or TPUs wired together, and the chips are the single largest line item.

Impact on the model: more compute allows bigger models trained on more data, which is the main driver of capability gains.

Amortized costs for frontier models such as GPT-4 and Gemini (Cottier et al., 2024).

Resource 3

Energy

Every GPU draws hundreds of watts around the clock, plus cooling. Energy is a small share of the dollar cost but the most visible environmental cost, and it is growing fastest in absolute terms.

Impact on the model: energy does not make a model smarter by itself, but power and cooling limits now decide how large a cluster can be built.

Resource 4

Time and people

A frontier run ties up a cluster for weeks to months, and hardware fails constantly at that scale. Around the run sit teams of researchers, engineers, and human raters whose salaries rival the chip bill.

Impact on the model: longer runs on more data improve quality, while human feedback is what turns a raw text predictor into a helpful assistant.

Part 5 · So what

05Key takeaways

Pretraining dominates the bill.

It is the step that consumes most of the chips, energy, and calendar time. Every other step is cheap by comparison.

Costs grow faster than budgets.

At 2.4× a year, the largest runs are projected to pass $1 billion by 2027, limiting frontier training to a handful of companies.

Energy is the hidden constraint.

It is only 2 to 6% of the dollars, but power and cooling now limit where clusters can be built and carry real environmental costs.

Transparency is uneven.

Only Meta publishes detailed training numbers. For investment or policy decisions, most figures are still estimates that should be treated with care.

For most organizations, build on, do not train.

Fine tuning or retrieval on top of an existing model costs a tiny fraction of pretraining, which is how most enterprises actually adopt LLMs.
